Competition shooting is the one discipline where caliber selection is driven almost entirely by the rulebook. Every format defines scoring criteria — power factor, hit factor, time, precision — that mathematically favor specific cartridges. Understanding why a caliber dominates requires understanding the rules that govern it. In USPSA, power factor (bullet weight in grains times velocity in fps, divided by 1,000) determines your scoring division. In PRS, wind deflection and the ability to spot impacts dictate cartridge choice. In Bullseye, precision at 25 and 50 yards with one-handed shooting makes recoil the enemy.

### [9mm Luger](/calibers/9mm-luger)

The 9mm dominates action pistol — USPSA, IDPA, Steel Challenge, and 3-Gun pistol stages — to a degree no other caliber approaches. USPSA Production and Carry Optics divisions require a minimum power factor of 125 (Minor), achievable with a 147-grain bullet at just 850 fps. That low bar means the 9mm can be loaded to barely clear the minimum while producing the softest recoil, the flattest dot tracking, and the fastest split times. Over a 150-round match, cumulative recoil savings over .40 S&W translate to faster times and more alpha-zone hits. Load 147-grain coated bullets to make 130 power factor with minimal recoil, and spend the savings practicing.

### [6.5 Creedmoor](/calibers/65-creedmoor)

The 6.5 Creedmoor is to PRS what the 9mm is to USPSA. PRS matches require engaging steel at 100 to 1,200+ yards from improvised positions under strict time limits. The 6.5 CM's low recoil (roughly 11 foot-pounds in a 14-pound rifle) allows shooters to spot impacts through the scope without losing the target. The 140-grain match bullets stay supersonic past 1,300 yards. Barrel life of 2,500-3,000 rounds covers a full season. Factory match ammunition from Hornady and Federal is accurate enough to be competitive without handloading. Equipment surveys consistently show the 6.5 CM in 50-60% of all PRS rifles.

### [.223 Remington](/calibers/223-remington)

The .223 Remington in an AR-15 is mandatory for 3-Gun rifle stages — near-zero recoil, 30-round capacity, and fast semi-auto fire make it overwhelmingly dominant. No serious 3-Gun competitor runs anything else. In NRA High Power and Service Rifle, the .223 has largely displaced the .308 thanks to its accuracy advantage with 77-grain and 80-grain match bullets at 200-600 yard distances and lower recoil during rapid-fire stages. For the competitor wanting one rifle caliber across the most disciplines, the .223's combination of low cost, low recoil, and high capacity makes it the most versatile competitive rifle cartridge.

### [6mm Creedmoor](/calibers/6mm-creedmoor)

The 6mm Creedmoor is for PRS competitors willing to trade barrel life for a competitive edge. The 105-108 grain bullets at 3,000+ fps produce roughly 15% less wind drift than the 6.5 CM at 800 yards, and recoil drops to 9-10 foot-pounds. The cost is 2,000-2,500 rounds of barrel life — meaning two changes per season for a high-volume competitor. At the club level, the 6.5 Creedmoor is the smarter investment. At the national level, the 6mm CM's marginal advantages have made it the most common cartridge among top-20 finishers.

### [.308 Winchester](/calibers/308-winchester)

The .308 fills two competitive niches: entry-level PRS and specified-cartridge military/LE matches. In PRS, its 5,000+ round barrel life and cheap factory match ammunition (Federal Gold Medal Match 175-grain SMK at $1.00-1.25/round) make it the most affordable way to compete. You will be at a ballistic disadvantage past 800 yards versus 6.5 CM shooters, but at the local club level, fundamentals matter more than cartridge selection. Spend the money saved on barrels and ammunition on match fees and practice instead.

### [.38 Special](/calibers/38-special)

The .38 Special loaded with 148-grain wadcutters is the Bullseye (Precision Pistol) cartridge. One-handed shooting at 25 and 50 yards makes recoil the enemy, and the wadcutter — flat-nosed lead loaded flush with the case mouth — produces barely 3-4 foot-pounds of felt recoil while cutting perfectly round scoring holes in paper. Revolvers like the S&W Model 14 have set records that still stand. If you reload wadcutters yourself, cost drops under $0.10 per round — the cheapest centerfire competition cartridge by a wide margin.

### [.45 ACP](/calibers/45-acp)

The .45 ACP is required for the centerfire phase of NRA Bullseye and remains strong in USPSA Single Stack (1911) division. In Single Stack, the .45 makes Major power factor (165 minimum) with standard-pressure loads, which means Major scoring — where a C-zone hit scores 4 points instead of 3 — with the softest possible recoil at that power factor. The 1911 in .45 ACP is also the most refined competition pistol platform ever built: a century of development has produced trigger actions and fitment tolerances that modern polymer pistols are only beginning to approach.

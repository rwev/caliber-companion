Choosing a home defense caliber means accepting tradeoffs that do not exist at the range. You are firing indoors, under extreme stress, possibly in the dark, with family members sleeping behind drywall. Overpenetration through interior walls becomes a primary concern, controllability under adrenaline matters more than raw ballistics, and rapid follow-up shots can be the difference between stopping a threat and missing entirely.

### [9mm Luger](/calibers/9mm-luger)

The 9mm is the best all-around home defense caliber. Modern hollow points — Federal HST 147-grain, Speer Gold Dot 124-grain +P — consistently expand to .55-.70 caliber and penetrate 12-18 inches in gelatin, meeting FBI protocol standards. That performance comes with roughly half the recoil of .45 ACP and 15-17 rounds in a standard magazine. When fine motor skills degrade at 3 AM, the 9mm's mild recoil lets you drive the gun faster and more accurately than any other effective defensive handgun caliber.

### [12 Gauge](/calibers/12-gauge)

A single round of Federal FliteControl 00 buckshot delivers eight .33-caliber pellets in a fist-sized pattern at 7-15 yards — the equivalent of eight simultaneous handgun hits. The tradeoffs are capacity (4-8 rounds), length (harder in hallways), and recoil. Reduced-recoil loads like Federal LE132-00 cut felt recoil by roughly 40% while maintaining terminal effectiveness. Pattern your specific gun with your chosen load at realistic distances — not all buckshot performs equally in all barrels.

### [.223 Remington](/calibers/223-remington)

This surprises people, but the ballistic science is clear: a lightweight, high-velocity .223 round actually overpenetrates *less* through drywall than 9mm or buckshot. A 55-grain bullet at 3,000+ fps is inherently unstable upon hitting a barrier — it yaws, tumbles, and fragments, losing energy rapidly with each wall. A 124-grain 9mm, by contrast, is a dense, stable projectile that punches through multiple layers. An AR-15 loaded with Hornady 55-grain V-MAX or Speer 64-grain Gold Dot offers devastating terminal performance with less danger to family members in adjacent rooms. The tradeoff is muzzle blast — an unsuppressed .223 indoors will cause immediate hearing damage.

### [.300 Blackout](/calibers/300-blackout)

The .300 Blackout's subsonic suppressed configuration is arguably the most refined home defense setup available. A 220-grain subsonic round through a suppressed AR-15 reduces the report to roughly 130-135 dB — hearing-safe with electronic ear protection. You retain the AR platform's ergonomics, capacity, and accuracy while eliminating the concussive indoor blast. Quality expanding subsonic loads like Hornady Sub-X 190-grain deliver reliable expansion and 14-16 inches of penetration. The catch is cost: ammunition runs roughly double the price of .223, and a suppressor adds $700-1,200 plus the tax stamp.

### [.45 ACP](/calibers/45-acp)

The .45 ACP's big, slow bullet starts at .452 caliber before expansion — Federal HST 230-grain routinely opens to nearly an inch in gelatin. Lower velocity (830-880 fps) means slightly less drywall penetration than hot 9mm +P in some configurations. The tradeoff is real: 7-8 rounds in a 1911, 10-13 in a polymer gun, versus 15-17 for a comparable 9mm, and substantially heavier recoil. The .45 earns its place for experienced shooters who train with it regularly and genuinely prefer the platform.

### [.380 ACP](/calibers/380-acp)

The .380 ACP is the minimum caliber worth considering, and it earns its spot specifically for shooters who cannot manage 9mm recoil — whether due to hand strength, arthritis, or recoil sensitivity. A gun you can shoot accurately beats a gun that intimidates you. Hornady Critical Defense 90-grain FTX and Federal HST Micro 99-grain both achieve reliable expansion from short barrels with 10-12 inches of gelatin penetration. That is the shallow end of the FBI window, but it is adequate when engagement distances are measured in feet.

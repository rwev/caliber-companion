The caliber you start with shapes your shooting habits for years. Start with too much recoil and you will develop a flinch — an involuntary anticipation of the shot that pulls your impact low and left and is extraordinarily difficult to unlearn. Start with something too mild and you may never build the confidence to step up to cartridges with real-world utility. These calibers form a deliberate progression, from near-zero recoil through centerfire cartridges that teach you to manage recoil without punishing you for it. Master these first, and you will shoot everything else better for the rest of your life.

### [.22 LR](/calibers/22-lr)

The .22 LR is the single most important cartridge in shooting, and it is the only correct starting point for a new shooter. This is not a suggestion — it is a foundational principle shared by virtually every professional instructor and military marksmanship program. The .22 LR generates roughly 0.5 foot-pounds of felt recoil in a rifle and barely more in a pistol, letting the shooter focus entirely on the fundamentals that produce accuracy: sight alignment, trigger control, breathing, and natural point of aim. None of those skills require a powerful cartridge, and all of them are degraded by recoil before proper technique is internalized. At 5-8 cents per round, you can fire 500 rounds for the cost of 50 rounds of 9mm. Buy a Ruger 10/22 rifle and a Ruger Mark IV pistol, shoot 2,000-3,000 rounds, and only then step up to centerfire.

### [9mm Luger](/calibers/9mm-luger)

The 9mm is the first centerfire handgun caliber a new shooter should learn, and for many it will be the last one needed. After building fundamentals with .22 LR, the transition to 9mm introduces real recoil — roughly 5-7 foot-pounds in a full-size pistol — without the sharp snap of more powerful cartridges. A full-size gun like the S&W M&P 9, Glock 17, or CZ P-10 F has enough weight and grip length to make the recoil feel like a firm push rather than a slap. Ammunition costs $0.25-0.35 per round for range FMJ, making high-volume practice affordable. Once you can put a full magazine into a 6-inch circle at 15 yards, you have a self-defense-ready skill set you can maintain for life.

### [.223 Remington](/calibers/223-remington)

The .223 Remington is the ideal first centerfire rifle cartridge. The AR-15 platform produces roughly 4-5 foot-pounds of felt recoil — less than most shotguns with target loads — while teaching the student to manage a proper rifle: shouldering, cheek weld, semi-automatic operation, and magazine handling. The flat trajectory out to 300 yards makes it excellent for learning distance shooting, and at 300-500 yards, wind drift becomes significant enough that the student must learn to read conditions — a skill that transfers to every other rifle cartridge. Ammunition runs $0.30-0.50 per round, affordable enough for regular practice. The .223/AR-15 is also the foundation of several competitive disciplines (3-Gun, Service Rifle), giving beginners a natural pathway into organized competition.

### [20 Gauge](/calibers/20-gauge)

The 20 gauge is the best shotgun gauge for beginners, and choosing it over the 12 gauge is the smarter decision. With target loads, the 20 gauge generates roughly 40-50% less felt recoil than a 12 gauge, and the lighter, smaller-framed shotguns fit younger and smaller-statured shooters better. For learning shotgun fundamentals — lead, swing-through, follow-through, and mount consistency — the 20 gauge teaches everything the 12 does without the bruised shoulder that drives new shooters away. It is fully competitive in sporting clays, skeet, and youth trap, and effective for hunting upland birds and small game. Start with a quality semi-auto or over/under — the Beretta A300 or CZ Redhead are excellent options — and shoot a case of shells before considering anything larger.

### [.243 Winchester](/calibers/243-winchester)

The .243 Winchester is the best first big-game hunting cartridge, bridging the gap between the .223's minimal recoil and full-power hunting calibers. Based on a necked-down .308 case, it pushes a 100-grain bullet at nearly 3,000 fps with roughly 10-11 foot-pounds of recoil — noticeably more than the .223 but substantially less than the .308 or .30-06. That level is manageable for most teenagers and adults with proper shooting habits, well below the flinch threshold. The .243 is genuinely effective on whitetail, pronghorn, and feral hogs with quality bullets like the 95-grain Hornady SST or 100-grain Nosler Partition. It also handles varmints and predators with lighter bullets, giving the beginning hunter a dual-purpose rifle.

### [.380 ACP](/calibers/380-acp)

The .380 ACP fills a specific role for beginners whose primary goal is concealed carry. For new shooters who come to firearms later in life, have smaller hands, or are recoil-sensitive due to arthritis or other limitations, the 9mm may feel like too much gun initially. The .380 in a pistol like the S&W M&P 380 Shield EZ — designed specifically for shooters with limited hand strength, with an easy-to-rack slide and easy-to-load magazines — produces roughly 3-4 foot-pounds of recoil. Starting with a .380 builds confidence and competence with a real defensive cartridge before stepping up to 9mm. Modern defensive ammunition like Hornady Critical Defense and Federal HST Micro provides adequate terminal performance. Many shooters who start here move to 9mm within a year as their skills develop.

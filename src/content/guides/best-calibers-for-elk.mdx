Elk are not deer. A mature bull can weigh 700-1,100 pounds, with heavy hide, dense muscle, and a thick shoulder blade that can stop an underpowered bullet before it reaches the vitals. The conventional wisdom of needing at least 1,500 foot-pounds of energy at impact is a reasonable guideline, but it is secondary to bullet construction. A premium bonded or monolithic bullet from a .270 will outperform a cheap cup-and-core bullet from a .300 Magnum every time, because the premium bullet holds together through heavy bone to reach the vitals.

### [.300 Winchester Magnum](/calibers/300-winchester-magnum)

The .300 Win Mag is the definitive elk cartridge. A 180-grain Nosler Partition at 2,960 fps delivers over 2,000 foot-pounds at 400 yards — comfortably above the 1,500 foot-pound threshold. It can drive 200-grain bonded bullets through the heaviest shoulder bones or push 180-grain Barnes TTSX clean through an elk at 300 yards. Recoil is stiff at 25-28 foot-pounds, and that demands honest self-assessment: if you flinch at the range, you will flinch on the mountainside. But if you can handle it, this is the elk cartridge all others are measured against.

### [.30-06 Springfield](/calibers/30-06-springfield)

The .30-06 has been killing elk since before most elk cartridges existed. A 180-grain Federal Terminal Ascent at 2,700 fps delivers roughly 1,700 foot-pounds at 400 yards — less than the .300 Win Mag, but inside 350 yards where most elk are killed, the distinction is academic. You get 20% less recoil, wider rifle availability, and cheaper ammunition. Load it with 180-grain Nosler AccuBond or 200-grain Nosler Partition and you are carrying more than enough gun.

### [7mm Remington Magnum](/calibers/7mm-remington-magnum)

The 7mm Rem Mag hits nearly as hard as the .300 Win Mag at long range with noticeably less recoil and a flatter trajectory. A 160-grain Nosler AccuBond at 2,950 fps carries over 1,700 foot-pounds at 400 yards, and the high-BC 7mm bullets resist wind deflection better than .30-caliber at comparable weights. The recoil advantage — roughly 22-24 foot-pounds versus 25-28 — means better practical accuracy. For mountain hunters who carry a rifle for miles at altitude before making one precise shot, this is an outstanding choice.

### [.270 Winchester](/calibers/270-winchester)

The .270 sits at the edge of what many consider adequate for elk. A 150-grain Nosler Partition at 2,850 fps carries approximately 1,400 foot-pounds at 400 yards — technically below the 1,500 guideline. But the .270 has been killing elk for nearly a century. The key is bullet selection (150-grain bonded or monolithic, not 130-grain deer loads) and shot discipline — broadside presentations inside 300 yards. Most hunters shoot the .270 extremely well due to its moderate recoil, and a well-placed .270 will always outperform a flinched .300 Magnum.

### [.338 Winchester Magnum](/calibers/338-winchester-magnum)

The .338 Win Mag is what Alaskan guides carry. A 225-grain Nosler Partition at 2,780 fps generates over 3,800 foot-pounds at the muzzle, and the heavy .338-caliber bullets penetrate through the heaviest bone structure an elk can present. Quartering shots, raking shots, shots through the shoulder — the .338 handles them all. Recoil approaches 30-35 foot-pounds in a typical 9-pound rifle, which is genuinely painful and demands serious range time. For thick timber where shots are close and angles are bad, this provides insurance that lighter cartridges cannot.

### [6.5 Creedmoor](/calibers/65-creedmoor)

The 6.5 Creedmoor for elk generates more arguments than any other modern caliber debate. A 143-grain ELD-X at 2,700 fps carries roughly 1,300 foot-pounds at 400 yards — below the traditional threshold. But the Creedmoor's accuracy, low recoil, and flat trajectory mean the average hunter places shots more precisely than with a magnum they shoot twice a year. The critical requirements: use the heaviest, toughest bullets available, wait for broadside presentations, and do not take quartering-to shots or attempt 500-yard shots where energy is marginal. With that discipline, it is legitimate. Without it, it is not.

### [.280 Ackley Improved](/calibers/280-ackley-improved)

The .280 AI is the connoisseur's elk cartridge — 7mm Rem Mag performance in a standard-length action with less recoil and better barrel life. The steepened 40-degree shoulder adds 100-150 fps over the standard .280 Remington. A 162-grain ELD-X at 2,900 fps carries over 1,700 foot-pounds at 400 yards. Factory rifles from Kimber, Nosler, and Browning are now available, and factory ammunition from Hornady and Federal is increasingly common. If you value efficiency and handload your own, the .280 AI is difficult to beat.

### [.35 Whelen](/calibers/35-whelen)

The .35 Whelen is the big-bore sleeper — zero internet hype, but a century of quietly killing elk in the timber. Based on the .30-06 case necked up to .358, it pushes a 225-grain bullet at 2,600 fps with 3,400 foot-pounds at the muzzle. The heavy .35-caliber bullets create massive wound channels, break shoulders, and anchor elk on the spot. The tradeoff is trajectory — this is a 250-yard cartridge at best. If your elk hunting happens in dark timber where shots are measured in yards, a 225-grain Nosler Partition or Barnes TSX from the .35 Whelen is devastatingly effective.

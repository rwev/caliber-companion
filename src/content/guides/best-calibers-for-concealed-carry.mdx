Concealed carry caliber selection is governed by constraints that do not apply to duty guns or range pistols. Your barrel is short — typically 3 to 3.5 inches — costing you 100-200 fps versus box velocities. Your grip is abbreviated, making recoil management harder. Your capacity is limited by what you can actually hide under a t-shirt. Every caliber on this list delivers reliable terminal performance from short-barreled pistols while remaining controllable enough for fast, accurate follow-up shots.

### [9mm Luger](/caliber-companion/calibers/9mm-luger)

The FBI spent years studying handgun terminal ballistics after the 1986 Miami shootout, and their conclusion was unequivocal: 9mm with modern hollow points is the optimal carry caliber. The reasoning is not about raw energy — it is about the intersection of terminal performance, controllability, and capacity. Federal HST 147-grain, Speer Gold Dot 124-grain +P, and Hornady Critical Defense 115-grain FTX all achieve consistent expansion and 12-18 inches of penetration from barrels as short as 3 inches, matching .40 S&W and .45 ACP performance because modern bullet engineering has closed the gap. The 9mm does it with 30-40% less recoil, meaning faster follow-up shots — and follow-up shots are what stop threats when first-round hit rates under stress hover around 30-50%. A micro-compact like the Sig P365 gives you 10-15 rounds in a package that disappears under a polo shirt.

### [.380 ACP](/caliber-companion/calibers/380-acp)

The .380 occupies a critical niche: it is the most effective caliber available in the smallest, lightest pistols. Guns like the Ruger LCP Max weigh under a pound loaded and fit in a pocket holster — which means they actually get carried every day. Older .380 JHP designs frequently failed to expand from short barrels, but that has changed. Hornady Critical Defense 90-grain FTX and Federal HST Micro 99-grain achieve consistent expansion to .50-.55 caliber with 10-12 inches of penetration. That is the shallow end of the FBI protocol window, but it represents genuine, documented improvement. The gun you carry every day beats the gun you leave at home.

### [.38 Special](/caliber-companion/calibers/38-special)

The .38 Special revolver remains viable for shooters who prefer the simplicity of a revolver's manual of arms — no slide to rack, no magazine to insert, no safety to disengage. The .38 Special +P with a 158-grain LSWCHP (the "FBI load") or Speer Gold Dot 135-grain Short Barrel has proven effective in real-world defensive shootings for decades. From a 2-inch barrel, expect 850-900 fps and 12-14 inches of penetration. The limitations are real — five rounds, slow reloads, heavy double-action trigger pull, and harsh recoil from lightweight alloy-framed snub-noses — but for the shooter who practices with the platform, the .38 +P is still a fight-stopper.

### [.357 Magnum](/caliber-companion/calibers/357-magnum)

The .357 Magnum is objectively devastating — a 125-grain JHP at 1,400+ fps from a 4-inch barrel produces terminal performance exceeding everything else on this list. The problem is that most of that performance evaporates from a 2-inch carry revolver. A snub-nose cuts velocity to 1,100-1,200 fps, and the muzzle blast and recoil are genuinely punishing. If you carry a .357 with a 3-inch or longer barrel — a Kimber K6s, Ruger SP101, or Colt King Cobra — you recover enough velocity to make the magnum loading worthwhile while keeping the package concealable. You also retain the ability to load .38 Special +P for practice.

### [.45 ACP](/caliber-companion/calibers/45-acp)

The .45 ACP in concealed carry is a niche choice for experienced shooters. The bullet starts at .452 inches, so even incomplete expansion produces a larger wound channel than a fully expanded 9mm. Federal HST 230-grain routinely opens to .85-.95 inches. The cost is capacity — 6-7 rounds where a comparable 9mm gives you 10-15 — and the .45's heavier recoil impulse demands a strong grip for fast follow-ups. Best suited to the experienced shooter who trains frequently and has consciously prioritized per-round terminal performance over capacity.

### [.30 Super Carry](/caliber-companion/calibers/30-super-carry)

Federal's .30 Super Carry fires a .312-caliber, 100-grain bullet at 1,250 fps, meeting FBI penetration and expansion standards while fitting 2-3 more rounds in the same magazine footprint as 9mm. A Shield Plus in .30 Super Carry holds 16 rounds versus 13 for the 9mm version. The downside is ammunition availability: production is limited primarily to Federal, and it costs roughly 50% more per round than 9mm. If the ammunition ecosystem matures, the .30 Super Carry has a genuine case as a 9mm alternative. For now, it is best suited to early adopters who buy in bulk.

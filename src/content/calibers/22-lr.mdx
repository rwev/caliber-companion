## Development & Design

The .22 Long Rifle was developed in 1887 by the J. Stevens Arms & Tool Company, created by combining the case of the .22 Long with the heavier 40-grain bullet originally used in the .22 Extra Long. This seemingly modest innovation — marrying an existing case to a proven bullet — produced what would become the most widely manufactured and consumed ammunition cartridge in the history of firearms. The engineering rationale was straightforward: the .22 Long's case provided adequate powder capacity, and the Extra Long's heavier bullet offered better ballistic performance and terminal effect on small game than the lighter projectiles then standard.

As a rimfire cartridge, the .22 LR uses priming compound spun into the hollow rim of the case rather than a separate Boxer or Berdan primer. This design makes the cartridge inherently inexpensive to manufacture — the case requires no primer pocket, flash hole, or separate primer component — but it also means the cases cannot be reloaded. The cartridge drives its 40-grain bullet at velocities ranging from roughly 1,050 fps in standard-velocity loadings to over 1,400 fps in hyper-velocity variants like the CCI Stinger, which uses a lengthened case and lighter 32-grain bullet. The .22 LR's overall dimensions are tiny: the loaded round is just under one inch long and weighs approximately 52 grains, meaning a shooter can carry 500 rounds in a pocket.

The .22 LR built upon a lineage of earlier .22-caliber rimfire cartridges. The .22 Short appeared in 1857 — developed by Smith & Wesson for their first revolver, the Model 1 — making it one of the oldest surviving metallic cartridge designs. The .22 Long followed in 1871, and the .22 Extra Long shortly after. The Long Rifle variant outlasted all its predecessors because its combination of case volume, bullet weight, and velocity represented the optimal balance for accuracy, terminal performance, and economy in a rimfire package.

## Military & Training Role

While never a primary combat cartridge, the .22 LR has played a critical supporting role in military training for over a century. During both World Wars, nations on every side used .22 LR training rifles to teach marksmanship fundamentals before transitioning recruits to full-power service rifles. The U.S. military issued millions of .22 LR training rifles, and the cartridge was standard for indoor range qualification at many installations. The British military used .22 LR conversions of the Lee-Enfield extensively for basic rifle training.

During World War II, the .22 LR found a more clandestine application. The Office of Strategic Services (OSS) developed suppressed .22 LR pistols — notably the High Standard HDM — for covert operations where silence was paramount. The cartridge's subsonic velocity in standard loadings made it naturally suited to suppressed use, a characteristic that intelligence and special operations communities continued to exploit in subsequent decades.

## Competition & Sport Use

The .22 LR is the foundation of competitive shooting worldwide. Olympic shooting events have used the cartridge since the early twentieth century, with disciplines including 50-meter rifle prone, 25-meter rapid fire pistol, and 10-meter running target all standardized around .22 LR ammunition. The precision demanded at this level is extraordinary — competitive shooters expect ten-shot groups well under half an inch at 50 meters — and has driven the development of match-grade .22 LR ammunition from manufacturers like Eley, Lapua, RWS, and SK.

Domestically, the .22 LR dominates NRA Bullseye pistol competition, the traditional American precision handgun discipline. The Steel Challenge shooting sport sees massive .22 LR participation thanks to dedicated rimfire divisions. Appleseed Project marksmanship clinics, which teach rifle fundamentals to civilians across the United States, use the .22 LR almost exclusively. In smallbore silhouette shooting, competitors engage metallic animal-shaped targets at distances from 40 to 100 meters, demanding both accuracy and wind-reading skill from the modest cartridge.

## Civilian Market Impact

The .22 LR is the highest-volume ammunition cartridge sold worldwide, with billions of rounds produced annually across dozens of manufacturers on multiple continents. Its appeal rests on three unbeatable advantages: extremely low cost, negligible recoil, and extraordinary versatility. A box of 500 rounds of .22 LR typically costs less than a single box of 50 centerfire pistol cartridges, making it the most economical path to developing and maintaining shooting proficiency.

The ammunition shortage of 2012-2014 demonstrated just how deeply embedded the .22 LR is in American shooting culture. When panic buying drained supply chains, the humble .22 LR was among the most difficult cartridges to find, with retail shelves stripped bare for months and prices spiking to five or ten times their normal levels. The shortage was a direct consequence of the cartridge's enormous consumer base: more people shoot .22 LR than any other single cartridge, and even a modest percentage increase in individual purchasing overwhelmed production capacity.

The cartridge has been chambered in virtually every type of firearm action ever devised. Semi-automatic pistols like the Ruger Mark IV and Browning Buck Mark are range staples. The Ruger 10/22, introduced in 1964, is arguably the most popular sporting rifle in American history. Bolt-action trainers, lever-action gallery guns, pump-action rifles, revolvers, derringers, and even belt-fed machine guns have been produced in .22 LR. Conversion kits for AR-15 platforms and popular centerfire pistols allow shooters to practice with their duty or carry guns at a fraction of centerfire ammunition cost.

## Notable Variants

The .22 LR exists within a family of related rimfire cartridges that share the same .223-inch bore diameter. The .22 Short remains in limited production for specific applications, particularly in Olympic rapid-fire pistol events (prior to rule changes) and as a low-noise pest control round. The .22 Winchester Magnum Rimfire (.22 WMR), introduced in 1959, uses a longer, more powerful case and is not interchangeable with .22 LR firearms, but serves as a step up in terminal performance for small game and varmint hunting. The .17 HMR, introduced by Hornady in 2002, necks the .22 WMR case down to .17 caliber for dramatically flatter trajectories and higher velocities, carving out a niche as a dedicated varmint cartridge.

## Future Outlook

The .22 LR's position as the world's most popular cartridge is unassailable for the foreseeable future. Its role as the universal entry point for new shooters, the most affordable training option for experienced ones, and a capable small-game hunting round ensures perpetual demand. Manufacturing capacity has expanded significantly since the shortages of the 2010s, and the cartridge's simplicity means it will remain among the least expensive ammunition options available. As long as people shoot, the .22 Long Rifle will be there — the first cartridge most learn with and the last one most give up.

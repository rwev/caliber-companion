## Development & Design

The .38 Smith & Wesson Special was born from a battlefield failure. During the Philippine-American War of 1899-1902, U.S. troops armed with .38 Long Colt revolvers found the cartridge unable to reliably stop determined Moro guerrilla fighters. Reports of combatants absorbing multiple hits and continuing to fight reached Washington and prompted an urgent reassessment of American military sidearm ballistics. The Army's immediate response was to reissue stockpiled .45 Colt Single Action Army revolvers, but the longer-term need was clear: a more effective medium-caliber revolver cartridge was required.

Smith & Wesson answered in 1898 (though production and distribution ramped up through 1899) with the .38 S&W Special. The design was straightforward: the .38 Long Colt case was lengthened from approximately 1.031 inches to 1.155 inches, increasing powder capacity and allowing a heavier powder charge behind a 158-grain round-nose lead bullet. The resulting cartridge produced a muzzle velocity of approximately 770 fps from a 4-inch barrel — a meaningful improvement over the .38 Long Colt's roughly 700 fps with a lighter bullet. The U.S. Army adopted it in 1899, chambered in Smith & Wesson Military & Police and Colt New Army revolvers.

The nomenclature is a perennial source of confusion for new shooters. Despite the ".38" designation, the cartridge uses bullets measuring .357-.358 inches in diameter, seated in a case with a rim diameter of .440 inches. The ".38" traces back to the original .38 Short Colt, whose case measured approximately .38 inches in diameter. This naming convention has persisted for over 125 years, surviving every opportunity for correction.

## Law Enforcement Dominance

The .38 Special's role in American law enforcement cannot be overstated — for roughly seventy years, from the 1920s through the early 1990s, it was effectively the American police cartridge. The Smith & Wesson Military & Police revolver (later designated the Model 10) became the most widely issued law enforcement sidearm in American history, with more than six million produced. The Colt Official Police, Colt Police Positive Special, and later the Ruger Service-Six and Speed-Six served in the same role.

The standard-issue load for most of this era was a 158-grain round-nose lead bullet at modest velocity — a configuration that produced adequate, if unspectacular, terminal performance. The limitations of this loading became increasingly apparent as law enforcement encounters with determined, armed suspects grew more frequent and violent in the 1960s and 1970s. Agencies responded by adopting +P loads — higher-pressure ammunition operating at 20,000 psi compared to the standard 17,000 psi — which drove the same 158-grain bullet approximately 100 fps faster and with improved hollow-point designs.

The FBI load — a 158-grain lead semi-wadcutter hollow-point +P, commonly known by its acronym "LSWCHP +P" and marketed by Federal, Winchester, and Remington — emerged in the late 1970s as arguably the most effective .38 Special defensive load ever developed. The soft lead projectile expanded reliably at .38 Special +P velocities, and the load earned an excellent reputation for real-world terminal performance. It remains in production and continues to be recommended by knowledgeable instructors as the benchmark .38 Special self-defense load.

The transition of American law enforcement from revolvers to semi-automatic pistols began in earnest in the late 1980s, accelerated by the 1986 FBI Miami shootout and the subsequent adoption of higher-capacity semi-automatics across the profession. By the mid-1990s, the .38 Special revolver had been largely displaced from frontline duty use by 9mm, .40 S&W, and .357 SIG semi-automatics. The transition was driven primarily by capacity — a revolver held five or six rounds versus 15-17 in a modern semi-automatic — and by reload speed, not by any fundamental inadequacy of the .38 Special's terminal performance.

## Military Service

The .38 Special served the U.S. military in various capacities from its 1899 adoption through the end of the twentieth century. During both World Wars, it was issued to aviators, military police, officers, and personnel in rear-echelon and specialist roles where a sidearm was required but a full-size service pistol was impractical. The Smith & Wesson Victory Model revolver in .38 Special was produced in large quantities during World War II, with many supplied to allied nations under Lend-Lease.

The .38 Special continued in limited military use through the Vietnam era and beyond, primarily as a backup or specialized weapon. Air Force aircrew revolvers, intelligence personnel sidearms, and security police revolvers were commonly chambered in .38 Special well into the 1980s, even after the M9 pistol in 9mm became the standard military sidearm.

## Civilian Market Impact

The .38 Special's civilian popularity has proven remarkably durable despite the cartridge's displacement from professional duty use. Snub-nose revolvers — the Smith & Wesson J-frame series (particularly the Model 642 and 442), the Ruger LCR, and the Kimber K6s — remain among the most popular concealed carry firearms in America. Their appeal rests on simplicity, reliability, and ease of use under stress. A revolver has no safety to disengage, no slide to rack, no magazine release to fumble — the shooter draws and presses the trigger. For many individuals, particularly those who carry a firearm for personal protection but do not train extensively, this simplicity is the decisive advantage.

The .38 Special also thrives as a target and recreational cartridge. Light wadcutter loads — a flat-fronted bullet seated flush with the case mouth at approximately 700 fps — produce minimal recoil and punch clean, easily scored holes in paper targets. These loads are favorites among bullseye shooters, training instructors, and recreational plinkers. The cartridge's inherent accuracy in quality revolvers is excellent, and the gentle recoil makes extended practice sessions comfortable.

## Relationship to .357 Magnum

The .38 Special's most significant derivative is the .357 Magnum, developed in the 1930s by lengthening the .38 Special case to prevent its chambering in older, weaker revolvers not designed for the higher pressures. This dimensional relationship created one of the most practical features in the handgun world: any .357 Magnum revolver can also fire .38 Special ammunition. This backward compatibility gives .357 Magnum owners access to mild, inexpensive .38 Special loads for training and practice, while retaining the option to load full-power .357 Magnum for defense or hunting. The arrangement benefits the .38 Special market as well, since every .357 Magnum revolver sold represents another firearm capable of consuming .38 Special ammunition.

## Future Outlook

The .38 Special occupies a paradoxical market position: displaced from professional use yet commercially vital. Snub-nose revolver sales remain strong, driven by the concealed carry market and the needs of shooters who prioritize simplicity and reliability above magazine capacity. The cartridge's role as the training and practice companion to the .357 Magnum sustains substantial ammunition demand. And the sheer number of .38 Special revolvers in circulation — likely tens of millions in the United States alone, accumulated over more than a century of production — guarantees a permanent market for ammunition.

Modern defensive ammunition from Hornady (Critical Defense), Speer (Gold Dot Short Barrel), and Federal (Hydra-Shok Deep) has improved the .38 Special's terminal performance meaningfully over the generic round-nose loads of decades past, making it a more credible defensive cartridge than its critics often acknowledge. The .38 Special may never reclaim its position as America's primary sidearm cartridge, but as a concealed carry, training, recreational, and backup cartridge, it remains indispensable — the quiet workhorse of the revolver world.

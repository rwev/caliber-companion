## Development & Design

The .40 S&W is one of the few cartridges whose origin can be traced to a single, specific event: the FBI Miami shootout of April 11, 1986. That morning, eight FBI agents attempted to stop two serial bank robbers — William Matix and Michael Platt — in a felony vehicle stop in the Suniland neighborhood of unincorporated Miami-Dade County, Florida. The ensuing gunfight left both suspects and two agents dead, with five additional agents wounded. The engagement lasted approximately five minutes, and forensic analysis determined that one of Platt's early wounds — a 9mm Winchester Silvertip that stopped just short of his heart after penetrating his arm — would have been rapidly fatal had the bullet penetrated slightly deeper. Platt continued fighting for several minutes after sustaining that wound, killing both agents.

The FBI's exhaustive post-incident analysis, led by the Firearms Training Unit at Quantico, concluded that the Bureau's 9mm and .38 Special service ammunition lacked sufficient penetration to reliably reach vital organs through intermediate barriers like arms, heavy clothing, and auto glass. This launched a comprehensive wound ballistics research program that would reshape American law enforcement ammunition selection for a generation.

The FBI initially adopted the 10mm Auto in 1989, partnering with Smith & Wesson to produce the Model 1076 pistol. However, the 10mm's full-power loads — pushing a 180-grain bullet at 1,300 fps — generated punishing recoil that many agents, particularly those with smaller hands, found difficult to manage. Qualification scores dropped, and the Bureau began downloading the 10mm to a reduced "FBI Lite" specification: a 180-grain bullet at approximately 950-1,000 fps.

This created an obvious question: if you were going to load the 10mm to reduced velocities anyway, why not shorten the case to fit in a more compact pistol? Smith & Wesson and Winchester answered that question with the .40 S&W, introduced on January 17, 1990. The cartridge used a case shortened from the 10mm's 25.2mm to 21.6mm, allowing it to fit in medium-frame pistols designed around the 9mm. The .40 S&W launched a 180-grain bullet at approximately 990 fps or a 165-grain bullet at approximately 1,090 fps — ballistics that closely replicated the FBI's preferred 10mm Lite loading in a package compatible with existing 9mm-sized pistol frames.

## Law Enforcement Adoption

The .40 S&W's adoption across American law enforcement was swift and sweeping. Glock introduced the Glock 22 — essentially a Glock 17 frame re-engineered for the .40 — and it became the most widely issued law enforcement handgun in the United States. By the mid-1990s, the majority of federal, state, and local law enforcement agencies had transitioned from 9mm pistols, .357 Magnum revolvers, or .38 Special revolvers to .40 S&W semi-automatics. The DEA, the U.S. Marshals Service, numerous FBI field offices, and the vast majority of state police agencies adopted the cartridge.

The .40 S&W offered a compelling value proposition for agencies making the pitch: it provided greater projectile diameter and energy than the 9mm, it fit in the same size pistols, and it offered higher capacity than the .45 ACP. For a brief period, it appeared to be the Goldilocks solution — not too small, not too big, not too much recoil, not too little energy.

## Civilian Market Impact

The civilian market followed law enforcement's lead, and by the early 2000s, the .40 S&W was one of the three most popular handgun cartridges in America alongside the 9mm and .45 ACP. Every major pistol manufacturer offered models in .40 S&W, and ammunition selection was broad. The cartridge found a dedicated following among concealed carriers and home defenders who wanted more energy than the 9mm without stepping up to the larger, heavier .45 ACP platform.

The .40 S&W also created an unintended benefit for budget-conscious shooters: as agencies transitioned away from the cartridge, enormous quantities of excellent-condition duty pistols — primarily Glock 22s and 23s, SIG P229s, and Smith & Wesson M&Ps — entered the used market at deeply discounted prices. These police trade-in guns represent some of the best values in the firearms market, often available for a fraction of their original cost in excellent mechanical condition.

## The Decline

The .40 S&W's fall from institutional favor began in earnest with the FBI's 2014 decision to return to 9mm. The Bureau's research concluded that advances in modern hollow-point bullet technology — particularly bonded and engineered projectiles from Federal (HST), Speer (Gold Dot), and Hornady (Critical Duty) — had closed the terminal performance gap that had originally justified the .40's adoption. The FBI's testing protocol, which evaluates penetration and expansion through a standardized set of barriers (heavy clothing, auto glass, plywood, wallboard, and steel), showed that top-tier 9mm defensive loads now met or exceeded the Bureau's minimum performance standards.

The calculus was straightforward: if 9mm defensive ammunition performs equivalently to .40 S&W in terminal ballistics testing, then 9mm's advantages — higher magazine capacity (typically 2-3 additional rounds per magazine), lower recoil (enabling faster and more accurate follow-up shots), reduced training difficulty, and lower ammunition cost — made it the objectively superior choice for a large organization. Agency after agency followed the FBI's lead. The trend accelerated through the late 2010s and into the 2020s, and by the mid-2020s, the .40 S&W had been substantially displaced from its former position as the dominant law enforcement cartridge.

## Competition & Sport Use

The .40 S&W has an active competitive niche in USPSA and IPSC shooting, where it is used to make Major power factor (165 minimum) in Limited, Limited-10, and Single Stack divisions. Shooters in these divisions benefit from the scoring advantage that Major power factor provides — hits in the C and D zones score higher than they would at Minor power factor — making the .40 S&W a competitive necessity for those who want to maximize their scoring potential in these divisions without stepping up to the more challenging recoil of the .45 ACP.

## Future Outlook

The .40 S&W's trajectory is one of managed decline rather than obsolescence. The cartridge still has an enormous installed base — millions of pistols in civilian hands and substantial law enforcement legacy stocks — and ammunition remains widely available from every major manufacturer. It will continue to serve shooters who prefer its performance characteristics and those who own .40-caliber pistols acquired during the cartridge's heyday. But the trend is clear: new law enforcement adoptions in .40 S&W have effectively ceased, new pistol model introductions increasingly omit the chambering, and the 9mm has reclaimed its position as the default service pistol cartridge. The .40 S&W achieved exactly what it was designed to do — it filled a perceived gap between 9mm and .45 ACP — but advancing bullet technology eventually eliminated that gap, and the cartridge's raison d'etre evaporated with it.
